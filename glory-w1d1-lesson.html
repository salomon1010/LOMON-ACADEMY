Two sentences walk into a room.
A conjunction introduces them.

Here is a sentence: "She failed the test."
Here is another: "She didn't study."

Both are fine on their own. But read them side by side and something feels missing — the relationship between them. Did the first happen because of the second? Although the second? Unless the second was actually false?

That relationship — cause, contrast, condition, time — is what a subordinating conjunction carries. One word changes everything: who is to blame, how surprising the outcome is, what would have prevented it.

Core Teaching — The Rule

What is a subordinating conjunction?

A subordinating conjunction is a word that connects two clauses — a main clause and a dependent clause — and shows the logical relationship between them.

The dependent clause cannot stand alone. It depends on the main clause to make sense. The conjunction is the bridge.

Structure: main clause + [conjunction] + dependent clause
— or — [conjunction] + dependent clause + , main clause

Demo — 3 Worked Examples
1
"She studied hard because she wanted to ace the test."
Relationship: Cause. The conjunction "because" tells the reader why she studied. Remove it and you have two disconnected facts. Add it and you have a reason.
2
Although it rained all afternoon, they finished the project."
Relationship: Contrast. Notice the comma — when the dependent clause comes first, you need a comma before the main clause. "Although" signals that the outcome was surprising given the condition.
3
"I won't stop trying unless someone gives me a good reason to."
Relationship: Condition. "Unless" sets up a condition that would have to be true for the main action to change. It shows determination — but with a specific exception built in.

The comma rule: When the dependent clause comes before the main clause, use a comma to separate them. When the main clause comes first, no comma is needed.

✓ Although it rained, they finished.
✓ They finished although it rained.

Recap + Bridge
💡
The one thing to remember: A subordinating conjunction doesn't just join two sentences — it tells the reader how those two ideas are related. Because = reason. Although = surprise. Since = time or cause. While = together. Unless = condition. Choose the right one and your writing thinks clearly. Choose the wrong one and the logic breaks.
